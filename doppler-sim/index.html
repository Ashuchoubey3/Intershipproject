Why does a siren drop in pitch the instant it passes you?

Most students assume the pitch fades smoothly from high to low as an ambulance goes by. Drag the sliders below and watch the wavefronts instead — the "drop" isn't gradual at all. It swings through the source's true frequency at one exact moment: the instant the source is directly beside you.

Frequency you hear, as the source travels past you

f(x) — heard frequency f₀ — true source frequency
i.

It's not the full speed that matters. Only the part of the source's velocity pointed straight at you (the radial component) changes what you hear. Off to the side, that component shrinks — which is why the pitch bends instead of switching instantly.

ii.

At the closest point, the radial speed is exactly zero. For one instant, the source is moving neither toward nor away from you — so you hear the real, unshifted f₀, right in the middle of the "drop."

iii.

Distance controls sharpness, not size. Move the "distance from track" slider — the high and low plateaus stay the same, but standing closer makes the swing between them almost instant. That's the "whoosh" you hear on a train platform.

Wavefronts above are emitted at a reduced, visually legible rate — not literal audio frequency — so the compression pattern stays readable on screen. All numeric readouts (Hz values, graph) use the true physics: f = f₀·c /(c ∓ v·cosθ), c = 343 m/s.
